    Grease Trap Hydro-Jetting in Brattleboro, VT

    Grease Trap Pumping and Hauling in Brattleboro

    Trap waste is regulated as a liquid waste, so in most places it has to be hauled by a licensed hauler to an approved receiving facility, not dumped down a manhole or into a dumpster. We do both the pumping and the hauling. Our vacuum trucks pull the full contents of the trap into a sealed tank, and the load gets logged with gallons and destination. No barrels left behind.

    Outdoor Grease Interceptor Pumping in Brattleboro, VT

    In-ground interceptors need a vacuum truck and usually a pressure wand to break up the cap. We open every compartment and pump each one to the floor. With the tank empty, we inspect the tees, baffle wall, risers and lid seals, since a cracked riser lets dirt and groundwater into the tank. Lids go back on tight.

    Hydro-Jetting for Grease Lines in Brattleboro, VT

    A cable machine pokes a hole through grease. Jetting actually removes it. We run a jetter at roughly 3,000 to 4,000 psi with a rotating nozzle that cuts the grease off the full circumference of the pipe and flushes it downstream. A camera pass afterward shows whether the line is really clean.

    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Brattleboro

    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. A large interceptor with a hardened grease cap can run past $1,000. Disposal is billed by the gallon at the receiving facility, so the gallons hauled drive most of the cost, and a 1,500-gallon tank fills a truck.

    Staff can clean a small under-sink trap between pump-outs. Disposal still has to be legal. Many kitchens have staff skim daily and book us to fully pump and scrape every few weeks, which keeps the interval long without skipping the records the inspector expects.

    FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. It pours as a liquid and sets up hard once it cools. Inside a sewer pipe it builds up in layers on the walls, traps food and wipes, and can shrink the opening to the size of a pencil.

    It goes to a permitted receiving facility. Some of it ends up as biofuel or digester feedstock. What is left gets dewatered and disposed of under the facility's permit, and each load is logged from your trap to their gate.

    Skimming off the top layer yourself is usually allowed. The disposal is the part that gets people fined. Many programs want a licensed hauler's record for every cleaning, and a kitchen doing its own has nothing to show the inspector.

    Under-sink traps take 30 to 60 minutes. Outdoor interceptors run one to three hours. Big tanks with a hardened cap take the longest, since the cap has to be broken up with a pressure wand before the hose will pull it.

    There is no parts warranty on a pump-out, since nothing gets installed. The work itself is guaranteed. Should it drain poorly after our visit, or a backup trace back to something we missed, the crew returns and makes it right.

    A small under-sink trap can be hand-cleaned by staff. Disposal is the hard part. Trap waste generally cannot go in a dumpster or down another drain, and an outdoor interceptor needs a vacuum truck and licensed hauler regardless.
