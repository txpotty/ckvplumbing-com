    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in Vermont

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 5 min read

    How a Tank Water Heater Works

    A standard tank heater keeps 40 or 50 gallons hot around the clock. It is simple and cheap to replace, and most plumbers can swap one in a few hours. The downside is standby loss. Heat leaks through the tank walls all day, so the burner or elements cycle even when nobody is using water.

    Some Vermont wells carry iron, manganese or other naturally occurring minerals, so a water test is the first step before choosing treatment.

    Tanks typically last 8 to 12 years. The anode rod inside sacrifices itself to slow corrosion, and once it is gone the steel tank starts to rust from the inside. A new anode every three to five years can stretch the tank's life. Few homeowners ever do it.

    How Tankless Units Work

    A tankless heater fires only on demand. It heats water as it flows through a heat exchanger, so nothing sits in storage losing heat. Endless hot water is the main draw for most people. The limit is flow, since a unit can only raise the temperature on so many gallons per minute.

    Incoming water temperature sets how much hot water a tankless unit can deliver. A unit rated at 9 gallons per minute might only manage 5 or 6 in winter, when the water coming in is much colder. That covers a shower plus the dishwasher, though two showers and a washer may be too much. Size for winter.

    Cost and Installation

    Swapping a tank is mostly a like-for-like job. Tankless installs usually cost more because the gas line and venting both change, and sometimes the electrical does too. A typical gas tankless unit draws 150,000 to 199,000 BTUs, more than many half-inch gas lines can carry. Upsizing to 3/4 inch is common.

    Venting changes too. A condensing unit vents through PVC, and a non-condensing one needs stainless vent. Our installers at CKV Plumbing check the gas line size and the vent route before we quote anything, so there are no surprises on install day. Condensing units also need a drain for the acidic condensate, usually through a neutralizer.

    New installations, water heater replacements and significant repairs often need a permit, especially in multi-unit and rental buildings.

    Either type needs a temperature and pressure relief valve piped to a safe spot. With a pressure reducing valve on the main, a tank heater usually needs a thermal expansion tank to go with it. Both kinds of install normally require a permit. We pull them.

    Which One Fits Your Vermont Home

    A tank still makes sense for plenty of houses. In a two-bath house where budget matters most, a same-size replacement tank is usually the easy call. Tankless earns its cost in houses that already run out of hot water, or where the tank takes up a closet you would like to reclaim. Space is a real factor.

    Tankless units often run 20 years with yearly maintenance, about twice what a tank lasts. On hard water, that maintenance means flushing the heat exchanger with a pump and vinegar or descaler once a year. Skip it and the unit will throw error codes within a few years. Gas units also need a clean air intake.

    Getting a Straight Answer

    Bring us the age of your current heater, the number of people in the house and whether anyone has ever run out of hot water mid-shower. Our plumber will check the gas line and the vent path, then price both options side by side. CKV Plumbing installs both types across Vermont, and you can reach the office at (802) 209-5139.
