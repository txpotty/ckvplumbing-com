    Slab Leak Warning Signs Every Vermont Homeowner Should Know

    By Aaron Mitchell · October 15, 2026 · 5 min read

    Pour a slab, lay soft copper or PEX in the gravel underneath, and you get a house that is cheap to build and solid for decades. A large share of homes from the last fifty years were built exactly that way. The catch is simple. When one of those lines opens up, nothing shows on the surface, and the water soaks into the base gravel for weeks before anybody notices. Catching it early keeps a pinhole from becoming a flooring and mold job.

    Why Slab Leaks Happen in Vermont Homes

    Under-slab copper fails for mechanical reasons first. A lot of homes from the 1970s through the early 2000s got soft copper laid right in the trench, with no sleeve, before the concrete went down. Over thirty or forty years, that tubing rubs against gravel and rebar every time it moves, and the wall wears thin at the contact point. The ground underneath makes it worse.

    Clay soils in river valleys drain poorly and can hold water against foundations, so sump pumps are common in low-lying homes.

    Expansive clay swells when it gets wet and shrinks back in a dry spell. That movement pushes the slab and the pipe in different directions. Buried stones and hard pan act as pressure points, and a copper line pinned against one of them takes a little more abuse each season until the wall gives out at that spot. Nobody sees it happen.

    Water chemistry does its share of the damage. Hard water and the chlorine or chloramine that utilities add for disinfection can pit copper from the inside, leaving tiny craters that eventually go all the way through. Hot lines usually fail first. They expand and contract every time a faucet opens, which grinds the pipe against the concrete.

    A large share of Vermont homes rely on private wells, and well water here can range from soft to fairly hard depending on the bedrock.

    The Warning Signs to Watch For

    Slab leaks almost never show up as puddles. Instead you get a string of small clues, each easy to explain away by itself, that point to water going somewhere you cannot see. These are the questions we ask on the first call:

    • A warm spot on the floor. A leaking hot line heats the concrete above it, and bare feet on tile usually find it first.
    • The meter moving with everything off. Shut off every fixture, the irrigation and the ice maker, then watch the small triangle or star dial on the meter for 15 minutes.
    • A bill that jumps for no reason. Compare it with the same month last year, since sprinkler season throws off month to month comparisons.
    • Running water you can hear under the floor or behind a wall when nothing is on.
    • A water heater that never seems to rest, because it keeps refilling with cold water to replace what is leaking out.
    • Damp carpet pad or cupped hardwood with no obvious source.
    • A musty smell along the baseboards, especially in interior rooms with no plumbing nearby.
    • New cracks in tile or drywall that show up alongside any of the signs above.

    One clue by itself proves nothing. Put two together, say a meter that creeps overnight and a warm patch in the hallway, and it is time to schedule leak detection before the water reaches the drywall. The 15 minute meter test alone catches a surprising number of slow leaks that had been running for a season or more.

    Finding the Leak Without Tearing Up the House

    Leak detection is a lot less destructive than most people picture. We start by isolating the hot and cold sides and pressure testing each one to see which line is losing water. Then the acoustic gear comes out, a ground microphone that picks up the hiss of water forcing its way out of a pinhole. On the hot side, a thermal imaging camera shows the heat trail of the leaking line right through the tile. Most of the time we can mark the spot within a foot or two before any concrete gets cut.

    Then you pick a fix. With a spot repair, we saw out a patch of concrete roughly two feet square, splice in new pipe and pour the patch back. A reroute abandons the leaking line under the slab and runs new PEX through the attic or walls instead. A full repipe makes sense after the second or third slab leak. Spot repairs often run about $1,500 to $4,000 depending on access and flooring, and a single-line reroute tends to fall somewhere from $1,000 to $3,500.

    What to Do in the Meantime

    Find your main shutoff today, before you need it in a hurry. In a lot of houses it sits near the front hose bib or in a curb box out by the street, under a lid you may need a screwdriver to pop. If the meter test confirms a leak and water starts surfacing, close the main. If the hot side is the problem, put the water heater on vacation or pilot so the burner or elements do not run with the supply shut off. Take photos of any damaged flooring. Many homeowners policies pay to access and repair the leak even when they exclude the pipe itself.

    When to Call a Professional

    This is not a weekend project. Cutting concrete without knowing exactly where the leak is wastes a day, and many newer slabs have post-tension cables that you do not want to hit.
