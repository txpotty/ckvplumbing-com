    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 6 min read

    The pipes you never see do some of the hardest work in the house. The water service line runs from the meter to the house, usually 3/4 or 1 inch pipe buried below the frost line. A sewer lateral carries everything from the drains out to the city main or the septic tank. Both are buried, often several feet down, and both can fail without much warning. These days a repair rarely means digging up the lawn from one end to the other.

    What Damages Buried Pipes in Vermont

    Tree roots cause more sewer trouble than anything else. Willows and silver maples are the worst offenders, though plenty of ornamental trees go after sewer lines too. A hairline crack or loose joint leaks warm, nutrient-rich vapor, and the roots follow it right in. Inside, they grow into a mat that snags waste and can eventually split the pipe.

    Rocky glacial soil and ledge are common across Vermont, which can make digging for water lines, sewer repairs or septic work slow and costly.

    Soil movement does damage slowly. Expansive clay swells in wet weather and shrinks in dry spells, while rock holds firm and creates pressure points. Over years that cycle shifts joints and cracks rigid pipe, and it leaves low spots called bellies where waste settles. Heavy trucks parked over a shallow line and poor bedding during construction add to the problem.

    Pipe material matters as well. Older neighborhoods may still have clay tile or cast iron sewer lines. Some houses also have galvanized steel water service lines that rust from the inside out. Homes built from the 1970s on usually have ABS or PVC sewer pipe, which does not corrode but can still crack, pull apart at the joints or flatten under load.

    Older homes often still have galvanized supply lines, cast iron drains or even lead service lines that deserve inspection and replacement.

    Warning Signs of Underground Pipe Damage

    Buried pipe problems show up indirectly, through other fixtures. Watch for these:

    • Repeat backups in more than one fixture, usually the lowest toilet or shower in the house.
    • A gurgling toilet when the washing machine drains.
    • Sewer smell in the yard or around a cleanout.
    • A green or soggy strip of lawn that stays green when everything around it is brown.
    • Sinkholes or depressions along the path of the line.
    • A water meter that keeps moving with every fixture off, pointing to a service line leak.
    • Low water pressure through the whole house.

    Modern Repair Methods

    Diagnosis comes first. On the sewer side, a waterproof camera goes in at the cleanout and records every foot of pipe out to the main. The footage shows roots, cracks, offsets and bellies, and a locator at the camera head marks the depth and location of each one from the surface. On a water service line, we pressure test it and listen with acoustic gear to find the leak.

    Cured-in-place lining slides a resin-soaked felt liner into the old sewer, inflates it and lets it cure into a new jointless pipe inside the old one. On a 4 inch lateral, the cured liner takes up very little of the inside diameter, and it flows better than rough cast iron did. It seals cracks and root entry points and usually needs only the cleanout for access. Pipe bursting pulls new HDPE through the line while a cone-shaped head breaks the old pipe outward. Bursting is the choice when the old pipe has collapsed or is too broken to hold a liner. Spot repair means digging up one section to swap out one bad joint or a single sag. For water service, directional boring runs a new line from meter to house with a small pit at each end.

    Cost comes down to footage and depth. Expect $150 to $400 for the camera inspection itself. Spot sewer repairs often land between $1,500 and $4,500, trenchless lining or bursting of a full residential lateral commonly runs about $4,000 to $12,000, and a new water service line is roughly $1,500 to $5,000. Per foot, trenchless can cost as much as digging or more, but it saves the driveway and landscaping from being torn out and rebuilt.

    When to Call a Professional

    Drains that keep backing up, or a meter showing a leak you cannot find, call for a professional inspection. Most underground jobs need a permit and an 811 utility locate, plus careful digging around gas and sprinkler lines.
