    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Winooski

    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. A large interceptor with a hardened grease cap can run past $1,000. The biggest variable is the gallons hauled, because disposal facilities charge by volume and a 1,500-gallon tank is a full truckload.

    FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. Warm, it pours like water, and cool, it sets like wax. In a sewer pipe it sticks to the walls in layers, catches food scraps and wipes, and eventually closes the pipe down to a pencil-sized opening.

    No permit is needed just to have a trap pumped. The records are your responsibility. A new or resized trap is different, usually needing a plumbing permit and sometimes a review by the pretreatment program first.

    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. The industry calls it brown grease. Yellow grease, the used fryer oil in your collection bin, is a separate product that gets recycled and should never go into the trap.

    It goes to a permitted receiving facility. Some of it ends up as biofuel or digester feedstock. The rest is dewatered and disposed of under the facility's permit, and every load we haul is logged so the trail runs from your trap to the gate.

    Slow sinks come first. Gurgling floor drains and a sour smell by the dish station usually follow. Gray water rising through a floor sink means the trap is far past 25 percent and grease is already moving downstream.

    Under-sink traps take 30 to 60 minutes. Outdoor interceptors run one to three hours. Big tanks with a hardened cap take the longest, since the cap has to be broken up with a pressure wand before the hose will pull it.
